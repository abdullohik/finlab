What Is a DCF? A Plain-English Guide

Discounted Cash Flow valuation, explained the way you'd explain it to a friend — then the formula, then the mistake that breaks it.

The one-sentence version

A DCF asks a single question: what is this company intrinsically worth, based purely on the cash it will generate in the future? Every other valuation method looks at what the market is currently paying for similar things. A DCF ignores the market entirely and tries to work out the "true" value from first principles.

Why a future dollar is worth less than a dollar today

The whole model rests on one idea: a dollar you receive in a year is worth less than a dollar you receive right now. Two reasons. First, you could invest today's dollar and earn a return, so waiting has a real opportunity cost. Second, there's always some risk the future dollar never actually arrives.

Say someone offers to pay you $100 in exactly one year. If you could earn 10% investing elsewhere, you'd only be willing to pay $90.91 today for that promise — because $90.91 invested at 10% grows to $100 in a year. That's discounting, in miniature. A DCF does the same calculation for every year of a company's projected cash flows, then adds them all up.

The three things every DCF needs

  • Free Cash Flow (FCF) — the actual cash a business generates after keeping the lights on and reinvesting in itself. Not accounting profit — cash. This is what gets discounted.
  • WACC (the discount rate) — the return investors require to put capital into this specific business. Riskier business, higher WACC, lower value today for the same future cash flows.
  • Terminal Value — the value of every cash flow beyond the explicit forecast period (usually year 10), stretching out into perpetuity. This is the input that decides the whole model, and almost nobody outside finance realizes how much it matters.

The formula

EV = Σ [ FCFₜ ÷ (1 + WACC)ᵗ ] + Terminal Value ÷ (1 + WACC)ⁿ
// Terminal Value, Gordon Growth Model:
TV = FCFₙ × (1 + g) ÷ (WACC − g)

EV stands for Enterprise Value — the value of the whole business to everyone who's put capital into it, debt and equity holders alike. Subtract net debt and you get Equity Value; divide by shares outstanding and you get an implied share price.

The part that surprises almost everyone

In a typical DCF, 60–80% of the total value comes from terminal value — not from the ten years of cash flows you actually projected in detail. That means the single most important number in the whole model is g, the terminal growth rate, which is usually just a small, mostly-guessed number around 2–3% (roughly long-run GDP growth). A tiny change here swings the final valuation by 20–40%. This is exactly why serious DCFs are always presented as a range with a full sensitivity table, never as one confident number.

The mistake that breaks the model entirely

Look at the terminal value formula again: TV = FCFₙ × (1 + g) ÷ (WACC − g). If someone sets the terminal growth rate higher than WACC, the denominator (WACC − g) goes to zero or negative — and the formula produces an infinite or nonsensical negative value. It's not a rare typo either: it's an easy mistake to make when you're optimistic about a fast-growing company and careless with the discount rate. WACC must always exceed terminal growth, full stop — it's the one hard rule in the whole model.

Where a DCF fits next to other valuation methods

A DCF is one of three main ways analysts value a company — the others being Comparable Company Analysis (what similar public companies currently trade for) and Precedent Transactions (what similar companies actually sold for in past M&A deals). No single method is treated as "the answer." Real valuations show a range from all three, and the analyst's actual job is explaining why they agree or disagree — not picking a favorite.
