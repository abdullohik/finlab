What Are Financial Statements?
Module 1 · Lesson 1 of 5 · 8 min read
📖 Learn
✅ Check
The Big Picture
What is a financial statement, and why does every banker read one first?
A financial statement is a company's official record of its financial activity — how much it earned, what it owns, what it owes, and how much cash moved in and out. Every public company publishes these every quarter and year. Analysts, bankers, and investors use these three documents to make every major financial decision.

When JPMorgan evaluates whether to lend to a company, they read its financial statements. When a PE firm considers buying a business, they read its financial statements. When you value a company in a DCF, every input comes from financial statements. Everything in finance starts here.
💡 Think of it this way
Imagine you're buying a used car from someone. You'd want to know three things: How much did they earn from using it? (Income Statement). What is it worth today, and does it have any loans on it? (Balance Sheet). Did it actually generate cash, or was it always being repaired? (Cash Flow). Same logic — just much bigger numbers.
The Three Statements
Three documents. Each answers a completely different question.
1
Income Statement — "Did we make money this period?" Covers a window of time (Q1, or the full year). Shows revenue, costs, and profit. Also called the P&L (Profit & Loss). This is what most people look at first — and what most people misread.
2
Balance Sheet — "What do we own and owe right now?" A snapshot at a single point in time — not a period. Shows assets (what the company owns), liabilities (what it owes), and equity (what's left for shareholders). Must always balance: Assets = Liabilities + Equity. Always.
3
Cash Flow Statement — "Where did the actual cash go?" Shows the movement of real cash — in and out. Often reveals a completely different picture from the profit numbers. The most honest of the three.
How They Connect
These three are not separate documents — they are one interconnected system.
Net Income from the Income Statement flows into Retained Earnings on the Balance Sheet every single period. This is how profitable companies build equity over time.

The Cash Flow Statement starts with Net Income from the Income Statement, then adjusts for every non-cash item to arrive at actual cash generated. The final net change in cash must match the change in the Cash line on the Balance Sheet.

If any of these links don't reconcile — something is wrong. Either an accounting error, or something deliberately hidden.
⚠ The most important rule in all of accounting
Net Income is not Cash. A company can report $200M of profit and simultaneously be running out of money. It can also report a loss while generating strong cash. This disconnect — between what is earned and what is collected — is the entire reason the Cash Flow Statement exists. And it is the most common place where financial problems (and outright fraud) are hidden.
🌍 Real Example — Luckin Coffee (2020)
Luckin Coffee reported strong revenue growth for years. But analysts who compared the Income Statement to the Cash Flow Statement noticed operating cash flow was consistently far below reported profits — revenue was being recognised before cash was collected. In April 2020 the company admitted it had fabricated $310M of revenue. The red flag was visible in the financial statements quarters earlier — if you knew what to look for.

Discounted Cash Flow (DCF)
Module 2 · Lesson 2 of 5 · 12 min read
📖 Learn
🔧 Calculator
✅ Check
Core Concept
What is a DCF and what question does it answer?
A DCF asks: what is this company intrinsically worth, based purely on the cash it will generate in the future?

The core idea: a dollar you receive in the future is worth less than a dollar you receive today. Two reasons: you could invest today's dollar and earn a return, and there's always risk the future dollar never arrives. A DCF captures both factors — discounting every future cash flow back to its present value, then adding them up.
💡 Simple analogy
Someone offers to pay you $100 in one year. If you could earn 10% investing elsewhere, you'd only pay $90.91 today for that promise ($100 ÷ 1.10). That's discounting. A DCF does this for every single year of a company's future cash flows — usually 10 years — then adds a "terminal value" for everything after.
The Three Inputs
Every DCF needs exactly three things.
Free Cash Flow (FCF)
The actual cash generated after maintaining and growing the business. Not profit — cash. = Operating Cash Flow minus CapEx. This is what gets discounted.
WACC
The discount rate. What return investors require to commit capital to this business. Higher risk = higher WACC = lower valuation today.
Terminal Value
The value of all cash flows beyond year 10, into perpetuity. Typically 60–80% of total DCF value. The most important and most dangerous input.
Enterprise Value (EV)
The output. Total value of the business to all capital providers. Subtract Net Debt to get Equity Value, divide by shares for per-share price.
The Formula
How the math actually works
EV = Σ [FCFₜ ÷ (1 + WACC)ᵗ] + Terminal Value ÷ (1 + WACC)ⁿ // Terminal Value — Gordon Growth Model: TV = FCFₙ × (1 + g) ÷ (WACC - g) // g = terminal growth rate (~2–3%, close to long-run GDP) // WACC must be greater than g — or the formula breaks // Bridge to Equity: Equity Value = EV - Net Debt Share Price = Equity Value ÷ Diluted Shares Outstanding
⚠ The Terminal Value Trap
In most DCFs, 60–80% of total EV comes from terminal value — not from the 10 years you projected. A tiny change in terminal growth rate or WACC moves the final answer by 20–40%. This is why DCF outputs are always presented as a range with a full sensitivity table — never as a single number.
How to build a DCF — 6 steps
1
Project Revenue & Margins — Forecast 5–10 years based on historical growth, industry trends, and management guidance.
2
Calculate FCF each year — EBIT × (1 − tax rate) + D&A − CapEx ± Working Capital changes. This is cash available to all capital providers.
3
Calculate WACC — Blend cost of equity (via CAPM: Rf + β × ERP) and after-tax cost of debt, weighted by capital structure. Typical range: 7–14%.
4
Calculate Terminal Value — Gordon Growth Model using a terminal growth rate, or an exit multiple applied to year-10 EBITDA.
5
Discount everything — Divide each year's FCF and the terminal value by (1 + WACC)^t. Sum all present values.
6
Bridge to equity — EV minus Net Debt = Equity Value. Divide by diluted shares for implied share price. Compare to market price.
💡 Senior Analyst Insight
At any top bank or fund, DCF is the theoretical anchor — never the only answer shown to a client. A DCF showing $2B when comps trade at $1.4B needs a clear explanation: either the assumptions are too aggressive or the market is mispricing. The analyst's job is to understand why the models disagree, not just run them.
🌍 Real Example — Why Tech Crashed in 2022
Zoom, Peloton, DocuSign lost 70–80% of their value in 2022 — not because their businesses collapsed, but because interest rates rose sharply. Higher rates → higher WACC → the same future cash flows are worth much less today. Companies where most of the DCF value was in distant future cash flows (high terminal value %) got hit hardest. Pure DCF theory playing out in real markets.

Move the sliders and watch the enterprise value respond. Pay close attention to WACC and terminal growth — notice how small changes produce large EV swings. That's the sensitivity problem every analyst has to manage.

The Leveraged Buyout (LBO)
Module 3 · Lesson 1 of 4 · 14 min read
📖 Learn
🔧 Calculator
✅ Check
Core Concept
What is an LBO and why does private equity use leverage?
An LBO is when a private equity firm buys a company using mostly borrowed money. The PE firm puts in a small equity check — typically 30–40% of the purchase price — and borrows the rest (60–70%) from banks and bond markets. The company itself then uses its own cash flows to repay that debt over 4–7 years.

The reason PE uses leverage is simple: it amplifies returns. Buy a $100 business with your own $100, sell for $120 — you made 20%. Buy the same $100 business with $40 of your money and $60 borrowed, sell for $120, pay back the $60 — you turned $40 into $60. That's 50%. Same business outcome. Dramatically different equity return.
💡 Mortgage analogy
Exactly like buying a house with a mortgage. Put down 20% ($40K), borrow 80% ($160K), buy a $200K house. House goes to $240K — you made $40K profit on your $40K investment: 100% return. Without the mortgage you'd have made $40K on $200K — only 20%. Leverage is the same mechanic, just applied to entire companies.
Three Value Creation Levers
LBO returns come from exactly three places — and they are not all equally legitimate.
1
EBITDA Growth (~45% of returns) — Revenue expansion, cost cutting, new products, bolt-on acquisitions. Real operational improvement. The most legitimate lever — real value created from the business itself.
2
Debt Paydown (~27% of returns) — Every dollar the business repays from its own cash flows transfers directly to equity value. Pure financial mechanics. No business improvement required. This is the financial engineering lever.
3
Multiple Expansion (~28% of returns) — Selling at a higher EV/EBITDA multiple than you paid. Can be market-driven or from transforming the business quality. Conservative models never assume this. Never build a return case that requires multiple expansion to work.
Return Metrics
PE firms measure returns with exactly two numbers: IRR and MOIC.
IRR
Internal Rate of Return. Annualised return on equity invested. PE target: 20–25%+. Below 15% = typically a failed investment that returns money but misses the fund hurdle.
MOIC
Multiple on Invested Capital. Exit equity ÷ entry equity. 3x means you tripled the money. Target: 2.5–3.5x. Below 2x = considered a failure regardless of IRR.
Hold Period
Typically 4–7 years. A 3x MOIC over 5 years ≈ 25% IRR. Same 3x over 7 years ≈ 17% IRR. Timing matters enormously. PE funds have a ~10-year life.
Entry / Exit Multiple
EV/EBITDA paid at purchase vs received at sale. Typical LBO entry: 8–14x. Conservative models hold entry = exit multiple. Multiple expansion is upside, never the base case.
⚠ Leverage amplifies losses too
If the business EBITDA falls 30% in a recession, the equity can be completely wiped out while the debt sits at full face value. The company still owes every dollar. This is exactly why JPMorgan's credit committee stress-tests every LBO against a severe downside scenario before approving the debt package — and why covenant structures exist.
🌍 Real Example — Blackstone / Hilton Hotels (2007–2018)
Blackstone acquired Hilton for $26B in 2007 — just before the financial crisis. Hilton's EBITDA fell ~40% in 2008–2009. Disaster scenario. Yet Blackstone generated ~$14B profit on exit in 2018 — the largest PE profit ever at that time. How? They renegotiated the debt during the crisis, installed new management, invested heavily in Hilton's Honors loyalty program, and expanded from 3,000 to 5,700 hotels globally. EBITDA more than doubled. Final return: ~3.0x MOIC, ~25% IRR over 10 years. The lesson: the best LBO returns come from real operational improvement — not financial engineering.

Adjust the deal parameters and watch IRR and MOIC respond. Try pushing leverage from 40% to 70% — watch the return jump. Then cut EBITDA growth to 0% and see how quickly the deal breaks. That's the stress test every credit officer runs.
